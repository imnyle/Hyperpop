Whole New World: Hyperpop as Queer Utopianism and the Importance of Radical Artmaking


Deep in Southwestern France, an army of 15,000-year-old painted bulls race across the uneven stone surface of tight cave walls. The Hall of Bulls, painted by paleolithic humans with a Michelangelo-esque eye for installation, is speculated to serve as a form of “hunting magic”: a ritual for ensuring successful future hunts (Looney). As evidenced by traditions starting in prehistory, it seems the desire to envision a brighter future is innate to human creativity.

Recent intellectual history has seen this desire materialize into a myriad of utopian schools of thought, often spearheaded by marginalized groups demanding social change.

When the imagination is confined by its circumstances, it “seeks refuge in wishfully constructed places and periods”
(Mannheim 184).

Perhaps most intriguing is how music, in both its content and form, has come to embody this utopian principle. From the anti-establishment punk music of the 60s and 70s to the reflective and racially galvanized hip-hop lyricism of the 80s and 90s, music has become a site for negotiating the future.
